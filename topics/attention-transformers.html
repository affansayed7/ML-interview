§15 · Deep Learning

Attention &
Transformers

Built up the way Jay Alammar teaches it — start with the black box, unpack one layer at a time, watch a single word compute attention, then zoom out to matrices. Everything downstream (BERT, GPT, your Mistral chatbot) is this same mechanism.

Companion reading This page is designed to be read alongside Jay Alammar's "The Illustrated Transformer" — his visualizations are the gold standard. This page gives you original diagrams in the same teaching flow, plus the derivations and interview follow-ups his article intentionally skips.

1. The high-level view — a black box

Start with the simplest possible picture: a Transformer is a function that takes a sequence in and produces a sequence out. For a translation model: "Je suis étudiant" goes in, "I am a student" comes out. Nothing more mysterious than that at the surface.

2. Cracking it open — encoders and decoders

Pop the lid: inside the black box is a stack of encoders on one side and a stack of decoders on the other. The original paper used 6 of each. All encoders have identical structure (though they don't share weights — each learns its own parameters); same for the decoders.

3. Inside one encoder — two sub-layers

Every encoder is exactly two things stacked: a self-attention layer, then a feed-forward neural network. The input to the encoder flows through self-attention first (letting each word look at every other word in the sentence to build context), then through the feed-forward network (applied independently to each position).

4. Bringing tensors into the picture — embeddings flow through

Now let's track what actually flows between layers. Words are first turned into vectors ("embeddings") of some fixed dimension — the paper used 512. A crucial property of the encoder: each position gets its own vector, and these vectors flow through the encoder in parallel. Word 1's vector goes through its own path, word 2's through its own, and so on — the only place they interact is inside self-attention.

5. Self-attention — the "it" example, word by word

This is the heart of the whole architecture, so let's build it up slowly with the classic example: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired."

As a human, you know instantly that "it" refers to the animal, not the street. But how would a neural network figure that out? When the model processes the word "it", self-attention lets it associate "it" with "animal" — by looking at every other word in the sentence and figuring out which are most relevant to understanding "it" in this context. This is what "attention" means: as the encoder processes any given word, it can peek at other positions for clues that help build a better representation of the current one.

The intuition in one sentence Self-attention is the mechanism that lets each word's representation be a weighted blend of every other word's representation — with the blend weights learned, so the model figures out on its own that when processing "it", it should heavily blend in information from "animal".

Step 1: Create Query, Key, Value vectors

For each word's embedding, we create three new vectors: a Query, a Key, and a Value. These are created by multiplying the embedding by three learned weight matrices — $W^Q, W^K, W^V$ — that are trained along with the rest of the model. In the paper, if embeddings are 512-dimensional, Q/K/V are each 64-dimensional (smaller, because we'll have multiple attention heads later; §6).

The three vectors have different jobs, and the names hint at a database-lookup analogy:

  • Query (q) — "what am I looking for?" — a representation of the current word used to search other words.
  • Key (k) — "what do I contain, in searchable form?" — used to be matched against incoming queries.
  • Value (v) — "what content will I actually contribute if matched?" — the payload delivered to whoever attends to me.

Step 2: Score each pair of words

Say we're computing self-attention for the first word, "Je". We need to score every word in the input against this word — the score tells us how much focus to place on other words when encoding "Je". The score is calculated as the dot product of the query vector with the key vector of the word we're scoring against.

So the scores when processing "Je" are: $q_1 \cdot k_1$, $q_1 \cdot k_2$, $q_1 \cdot k_3$, and so on. High dot product = high alignment = "these words are relevant to each other."

Step 3: Scale by √dₖ, then softmax

Divide each score by $\sqrt{d_k}$ (where $d_k$ = key dimension, 64 in the paper, so $\sqrt{d_k} = 8$). This is a crucial detail — without it, dot products grow large in magnitude as dimensionality increases, which pushes softmax into a regime where its outputs become nearly one-hot and its gradients nearly zero. Scaling by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps values in a range where softmax stays well-behaved and gradients continue flowing.

Then pass the scaled scores through softmax. This normalizes them so they're all positive and sum to 1 — these are our attention weights.

Step 4: Multiply values by weights, then sum

Multiply each word's value vector by that word's attention weight. Then sum all the weighted value vectors. The result — a single vector, same shape as any of the values — is the self-attention output for the position we were processing ("it"). This output is dominated by content from "animal" (weight 0.72) with a smaller contribution from "tired" (0.24), and essentially nothing from the rest.

The four steps, as one formula (scaled dot-product attention)
$$ z_i = \sum_j \left[\text{softmax}\left(\frac{q_i \cdot k_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\right] v_j $$
And all at once, in matrix form
$$ \text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V $$

The reason it's usually written in matrix form is efficiency: rather than computing one word at a time, we stack all embeddings into a matrix $X$, project them all at once ($Q = XW^Q$, $K = XW^K$, $V = XW^V$), and compute all attention outputs in a single matrix multiplication. This is exactly why Transformers are so much faster to train than RNNs — the entire sequence's attention can be computed in parallel on a GPU.

6. Multi-head attention — many attentions in parallel

The paper improves this basic mechanism by adding multiple attention heads. Instead of one attention computation, do it several times in parallel — each with its own separate $W^Q, W^K, W^V$ matrices. The original paper used 8 heads.

Why? Because one attention layer computes a single weighted average, which forces it to average across all the different types of relationships in language (coreference, syntax, semantics, ...) into one blend. Multiple heads let the model attend to different relationship types simultaneously — head 1 might specialize in tracking coreference ("it → animal"), while head 3 tracks the subject-verb relationship, etc.

Multi-head attention
$$ \text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, ..., \text{head}_h)\,W^O $$ $$ \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(QW_i^Q,\ KW_i^K,\ VW_i^V) $$

7. Positional encoding — injecting order

Notice: nothing in what we've built so far cares about word order. If you shuffled the input words, self-attention would produce the same set of outputs, just reordered. But of course, order matters — "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" mean very different things.

The fix: add a positional encoding vector to each word's embedding before the first encoder. These vectors use fixed patterns of sine and cosine functions at different frequencies, so every position gets a unique "fingerprint" that the model can use to figure out where each word sits.

Sinusoidal positional encoding
$$ PE_{(pos, 2i)} = \sin\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{model}}}\right) $$ $$ PE_{(pos, 2i+1)} = \cos\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{model}}}\right) $$

Why sinusoids specifically? Two reasons: they're bounded (so they don't blow up for long sequences the way raw position numbers would), and the specific math means $PE_{pos+k}$ can be expressed as a linear function of $PE_{pos}$ — which makes it easy for the model to learn to attend by relative position, not just absolute.

8. Residual connections + Layer Normalization

Each sub-layer (both self-attention and the FFN) is wrapped with a residual connection and layer normalization: the sub-layer's output is added to its own input, then normalized:

$$ \text{output} = \text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x)) $$

The residual connection gives gradients a direct highway to flow backward through the entire stack — same idea that makes ResNet trainable. LayerNorm (as opposed to BatchNorm) is chosen because it normalizes across the feature dimension per example, not across the batch, so it's insensitive to batch size and to variable sequence lengths — both crucial for NLP.

9. The decoder side

Decoders are similar to encoders, with two differences:

  1. They have an extra sub-layer in the middle: encoder-decoder attention. This is a normal attention layer, except its Queries come from the decoder's current state while its Keys and Values come from the top encoder's output. This is the mechanism by which the decoder decides which parts of the input sentence to focus on when generating each output word.
  2. The decoder's own self-attention is masked: when computing attention for position $t$, all positions later than $t$ have their scores set to $-\infty$ before softmax (so they end up with attention weight exactly 0). This prevents the decoder from cheating by looking at future tokens it hasn't generated yet — essential because at inference, those tokens genuinely don't exist yet.

10. Final linear + softmax layer

After the decoder stack produces its output vector for the current position, one final linear layer projects it into a much larger vector — one entry per word in the vocabulary. Softmax turns that into a probability distribution over the vocabulary. The output word is the argmax (greedy decoding) or a sample from the distribution (temperature-based sampling used in modern LLMs).

Interview Q&A

Walk through self-attention step by step, using the Query/Key/Value framing.

For each word's embedding, project it into three vectors — Query, Key, and Value — using three learned weight matrices. To compute the output for a given word, take the dot product of its Query with every word's Key (itself included), giving a raw similarity score per word. Scale these scores by $\sqrt{d_k}$, pass them through softmax to get attention weights summing to 1, then take the weighted sum of every word's Value vector using those weights. The result is a new representation of the word that has absorbed relevant context from across the sequence.

Likely follow-up "Why three separate projections instead of using the raw embedding as Q, K, and V?" — because the three projections let the model learn to represent "what I'm searching for" separately from "how I'll be found" separately from "what content I'll contribute" — using one shared vector for all three would tightly couple these roles and constrain what the model can learn.
Why scale the dot product by √dₖ before softmax?

As the key dimension $d_k$ grows, dot products between Q and K vectors grow in magnitude, since you're summing more terms. Large inputs to softmax produce a peaked, near one-hot distribution, and the gradient of softmax in that saturated regime is tiny — this stalls learning. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the softmax input in a range where gradients remain useful.

Likely follow-up "Why specifically √dₖ, not dₖ or some other function?" — if you assume Q and K components are independent with roughly unit variance, the variance of their dot product scales with $d_k$; dividing by its square root normalizes the variance back to approximately 1.
Why use multiple attention heads instead of one bigger attention?

A single attention head is one weighted average over the sequence — so it has to compress all types of relationships (coreference, syntax, semantic similarity) into one blend. Multiple heads with independent Q/K/V projections let the model attend to several relationship types in parallel — one head might specialize in tracking pronoun references, another in subject-verb agreement — and concatenating them preserves that diversity instead of averaging it away.

Likely follow-up "Does more heads always help?" — no, there are diminishing and eventually negative returns; too many heads with too little per-head dimension undermines any single head's ability to represent a relationship. It's an empirical hyperparameter.
Why do Transformers need positional encoding at all?

Self-attention treats its input as a set, not a sequence — shuffling the input positions just reorders the outputs, without changing them meaningfully. So the model has no natural way to know that "dog bites man" is different from "man bites dog". Positional encoding adds a unique, order-dependent vector to each token's embedding before the first layer, giving the model information about position that it can then use however it learns to.

Likely follow-up "Why sine/cosine instead of just adding position numbers?" — sinusoids are bounded (won't blow up for long sequences), and their structure means $PE_{pos+k}$ is a linear function of $PE_{pos}$, which makes it easy for the model to learn attention over relative positions, not just absolute ones.
What's the purpose of masking in the decoder's self-attention?

At inference, the decoder generates one token at a time and genuinely doesn't have access to future tokens. To keep training consistent with inference, the decoder's self-attention masks out future positions by setting their scores to $-\infty$ before softmax (making their attention weight exactly 0 after). Without this mask, the model would learn to "cheat" during training by peeking at answers and fail catastrophically at real generation time.

Likely follow-up "Is this why BERT doesn't use this mask?" — right; BERT is trained on masked language modeling (predicting randomly masked tokens with full bidirectional context), not autoregressive generation, so it uses unmasked bidirectional self-attention over the entire input instead.
What is encoder-decoder attention, and how does it differ from self-attention?

In self-attention, Q, K, and V all come from the same sequence. In encoder-decoder attention, the Queries come from the decoder's current state, while Keys and Values come from the top encoder's output. This lets each decoder position "look back" at the entire input sequence to decide what's relevant for generating the next output token — this is literally the mechanism that connects input to output in translation.

Likely follow-up "Is encoder-decoder attention masked?" — no; the decoder is allowed to see the entire input sequence (that's the point). The mask only applies to the decoder's own previously-generated outputs.
Why LayerNorm rather than BatchNorm in Transformers?

BatchNorm normalizes across the batch dimension per feature, which needs meaningful batch statistics and behaves inconsistently with variable-length sequences and small batches (both common in NLP). LayerNorm normalizes across the feature dimension per individual example — insensitive to batch size and sequence length — a much better fit.

Likely follow-up "Where in each sub-layer are the residual + LayerNorm applied?" — as $\text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x))$: sub-layer output is added to its own input (residual), then the sum is normalized.
What's the computational complexity of self-attention w.r.t. sequence length?

Self-attention scores every pair of positions, so it's $O(n^2)$ in time and memory with respect to sequence length $n$ — compared to an RNN's $O(n)$ per-step (though sequential) processing. This quadratic scaling is why long-context windows are expensive, and it's the direct motivation behind efficient-attention research (sparse, linear, sliding-window attention) used in long-context LLMs.

Likely follow-up "When would an RNN actually still be preferable to a Transformer?" — for very long sequences under tight memory/latency constraints (real-time streaming), where RNNs' constant per-step memory beats attention's quadratic blow-up.
Encoder-only vs decoder-only vs encoder-decoder — what's the difference and an example of each?

Encoder-only (e.g. BERT) uses unmasked bidirectional self-attention — good for understanding tasks (classification, NER) where the full input is known upfront. Decoder-only (e.g. GPT, Mistral 7B, LLaMA) uses masked causal self-attention only — built for autoregressive generation, one token at a time. Encoder-decoder (e.g. original Transformer, T5) uses both with cross-attention — natural for sequence-to-sequence tasks like translation or summarization.

Likely follow-up "Which category is Mistral 7B and why does that matter for your project?" — decoder-only, like GPT; that's why it's suited for open-ended chat/generation rather than the fill-in-the-blank tasks BERT excels at — this feeds directly into §20's Applied LLMs section.
Why did Transformers replace RNNs/LSTMs for NLP?

Two big reasons. First, RNNs must process tokens strictly sequentially — you can't compute step $t+1$ before step $t$ — so training can't be parallelized across the sequence dimension; Transformers process all positions simultaneously via matrix multiplication. Second, even LSTMs struggle with very long-range dependencies (gradients degrade over many steps), while self-attention gives every token a direct one-hop connection to every other token regardless of distance, making long-range relationships far easier to learn.

Likely follow-up "But attention is $O(n^2)$ — RNNs are only $O(n)$. Isn't that worse?" — per-step, yes, but Transformer training parallelizes across the sequence, so wall-clock time on GPUs is dramatically lower for typical sequence lengths, and the quadratic cost only starts hurting at very long contexts — where efficient-attention variants take over.
